Do you need a permit for a basement renovation in Ontario?

Almost always, yes — and skipping it can cost you far more than the permit fee. Here's what Ontario's Building Code actually requires.

By the NEVACO BUILD Team · September 24, 2026

In almost every Ontario municipality, a basement renovation that adds a bedroom, a bathroom, a kitchen, or a separate living space requires a building permit — and in most cases, an electrical permit (ESA) and a plumbing permit as well. Simple cosmetic work, like painting or replacing flooring with no structural or systems changes, generally doesn't. The line is drawn at anything that changes the basement's use, layout, or life-safety features.

What triggers a permit requirement

Adding a bedroom. Any room used for sleeping must meet Ontario Building Code egress requirements — meaning a window large enough and low enough to the floor to allow emergency exit, and a smoke alarm with interconnection to the rest of the home.

Adding a bathroom or a second kitchen. New plumbing rough-in, drainage, and venting need to be inspected, and a second kitchen can trigger additional fire-separation requirements if the basement will function as a secondary suite.

Framing new walls or altering the ceiling. New partition walls, especially ones enclosing a furnace or electrical panel, have specific clearance and fire-rating requirements.

Underpinning or lowering the floor. If the ceiling height doesn't meet minimum requirements for habitable space, underpinning to lower the slab is itself a permitted structural undertaking requiring an engineer.

Egress windows: the most commonly missed requirement

This is the single biggest reason basement renovations fail inspection. For a room to legally count as a bedroom, Ontario's code requires a window (or door) that opens directly to the outside with a minimum clear opening size and a maximum sill height from the floor — specifics that vary slightly by municipality and by whether the window serves a single room or a whole basement suite. If your existing basement windows are small hoppers or high on the wall, cutting a proper egress window (often meaning excavation outside the foundation) is frequently part of the project scope, not an afterthought.

What happens if you skip the permit

Unpermitted basement work creates real problems beyond the risk of a stop-work order or fine. It becomes a disclosure issue when you sell — buyers' lawyers and home inspectors routinely flag basement bedrooms and bathrooms with no permit history, which can delay a sale or force the buyer to negotiate a price reduction. Insurance claims involving fire or water damage can also be denied if the work wasn't permitted and doesn't meet code, since the insurer can argue the loss was made worse by non-compliant construction. And if you ever want to legally rent the basement as a secondary suite, unpermitted work has to be opened up and redone before it can be registered.

How the permit process actually works

Your contractor (or you, if self-managing) submits drawings to your municipality's building department showing the proposed layout, egress, and any structural changes. Once approved, work proceeds with scheduled inspections at key stages — typically framing, plumbing/electrical rough-in, insulation, and final. A licensed contractor who pulls permits regularly in your municipality can usually tell you realistic timelines, since approval times vary significantly between GTA municipalities.
