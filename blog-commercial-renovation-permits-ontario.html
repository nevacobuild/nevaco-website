Commercial Renovation Permits in Ontario: What GTA Business Owners Need to Know

When you need a building permit, what a change-of-use application involves, and how to keep approvals from delaying your project.

By the NEVACO BUILD Team · October 9, 2026

Renovating a commercial space in Ontario — whether it’s an office, retail store, restaurant, or medical clinic — almost always triggers permit requirements that residential projects don’t face. Skipping this step, or misjudging what applies to your space, is one of the most common ways commercial projects in the GTA lose weeks or months. Here’s what to plan for.

When you need a building permit

Under the Ontario Building Code, most commercial renovations require a permit if the work involves structural changes, new or relocated walls, changes to exits or fire separations, new mechanical or electrical systems, or any change to how the space is used (a “change of use”). A simple cosmetic refresh — new paint, flooring, or fixtures with no structural or systems work — may not require a permit, but it’s worth confirming with your municipality before work starts, since requirements vary by city within the GTA.

Change of use: the step owners often miss

If your renovation changes the occupancy classification of the space — for example, converting a retail unit into a restaurant, or an office into a medical clinic — you’ll likely need a change-of-use permit, separate from a standard building permit. This triggers a review against current Ontario Building Code requirements for that occupancy type, covering things like fire separation, exits, accessibility, and ventilation, even in parts of the building you’re not physically touching. This is the step that catches business owners off guard: a change of use can require upgrades to areas outside your renovation scope just to bring the whole space up to current code for its new purpose.

Accessibility requirements (AODA)

Commercial renovations in Ontario also need to account for the Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act (AODA) and the accessibility provisions in the Ontario Building Code. Depending on the scope of work, this can mean accessible entrances, washroom upgrades, and clear floor space requirements. Building this into the design phase is far less expensive than retrofitting after construction starts.

What the permit process typically involves

  • Zoning compliance check — confirming your intended use is permitted at that address
  • Drawings — architectural and, depending on scope, mechanical/electrical drawings sealed by a licensed professional
  • Application submission — to the local municipality (City of Toronto, Mississauga, Vaughan, Markham, etc. — each has its own portal and timelines)
  • Plan review — municipal review against the Ontario Building Code, zoning bylaws, and fire code
  • Permit issuance
  • Inspections — at defined stages during construction, before walls close up and systems are concealed

Timelines vary significantly by municipality and project complexity — a cosmetic tenant improvement can move much faster than a full change-of-use application. Build permit review time into your project schedule from day one, not as an afterthought.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit for a cosmetic commercial renovation?

Often not, if there’s no structural, mechanical, or electrical work and no change of use — but confirm with your local municipality before starting, since requirements differ across GTA cities.

How long does a commercial building permit take in the GTA?

It varies by municipality and project complexity. A straightforward tenant improvement typically moves faster than a change-of-use application, which requires a fuller code review.

What’s the difference between a building permit and a change-of-use permit?

A building permit covers physical construction work. A change-of-use permit is required when the function of the space changes (e.g., retail to restaurant), even if little or no construction is involved, because the new use must meet current code requirements.
